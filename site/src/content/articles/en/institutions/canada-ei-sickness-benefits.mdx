EI sickness benefits were designed for the flu that turned into pneumonia, or the surgery that needs
three months of recovery. They pay for a while and then you go back to work.

Parkinson's does not work that way, and the mismatch is the whole problem. You can still claim the
26 weeks — being unable to work for medical reasons is the test, and it does not care whether you will
recover. But you should claim them knowing what they are: **a bridge, not a destination.** The most
useful thing you can do during those 26 weeks is get the next thing decided.

This page covers EI sickness benefits and the caregiving benefits people often assume they can also
use. For the disability pension on the other side of the bridge, see
[Canada's federal disability benefits](/en/institutions/canada-disability-benefits/).

## What EI sickness benefits actually pay

Service Canada's own summary: benefits "can provide you with up to 26 weeks of financial assistance if
you can't work for medical reasons. You could receive 55% of your earnings up to a maximum of $729 a
week."

| | 2026 |
| --- | --- |
| Maximum weeks | 26 |
| Rate | 55% of average insurable weekly earnings |
| Weekly maximum | **$729** |
| Waiting period | 1 week, unpaid |
| First payment | about 28 days after you apply |

The calculation uses your **best weeks** — the highest-paid weeks in the 52 weeks before your claim.
How many best weeks are used depends on the unemployment rate where you live, and it "could be between
14 and 22 weeks."

That detail matters more for Parkinson's than it looks. If you spent the last year gradually cutting
your hours, the best-weeks rule works in your favour: Service Canada picks your strongest weeks, not
your average. It does not pick your strongest weeks from three years ago, though — the window is 52
weeks, so a long slow decline still lowers what you get.

There is also a **family supplement** if "your annual net family income is $25,921 or less," you have
at least one child under 18, and you or your spouse receive the Canada Child Benefit. It is added
automatically, and the total still cannot exceed $729 a week.

## Who qualifies

Three things have to be true, in Service Canada's wording:

> - you're unable to work for medical reasons
> - your regular weekly earnings from work have decreased by more than 40% for at least one week
> - you accumulated 600 insured hours of work in the 52 weeks before the start of your claim or since
>   the start of your last claim, whichever is shorter

The 600 hours is the requirement that quietly disqualifies people. Six hundred hours is roughly fifteen
full-time weeks. If Parkinson's had already pushed you down to two days a week for the past year, you
may not reach it — and the hours have to be **insured** hours, which self-employment generally is not
unless you registered for the EI special benefits programme for self-employed people.

One more condition applies while you are being paid: "you must remain otherwise available for work, if
it weren't for your medical condition."

<Callout text="Check for a recent claim before you count on 26 weeks. If you received any EI benefits in the past 52 weeks, Service Canada says you may not be eligible for the maximum number of weeks of sickness benefits. Your <b>claim period</b> is normally 52 weeks in total, whatever type of benefit you drew during it." />

## Four weeks is the deadline nobody mentions

Service Canada's instruction on the application page is one line, in bold, and it is easy to read past:
**"Apply as soon as possible after you stop working. If you apply more than 4 weeks after your last day
of work, you may lose benefits."**

Four weeks is exactly the period in which someone newly diagnosed is waiting for a specialist
appointment, waiting for a medical certificate, or waiting to see whether a medication change makes work
possible again. All three are good reasons to wait, and all three can cost you weeks of benefit.

Service Canada removes the excuse itself: **"Don't wait until you have the documents to apply. Complete
and submit your online application right away. You can send the required documents after you apply."**

<Callout text="Apply first, document second. The application needs your employers and dates for the past 52 weeks, your SIN, one of your parents' last name at birth, and your banking details for direct deposit — <b>not</b> the medical certificate. That can follow." />

## The medical certificate: more people can sign it than you think

You need a certificate "signed by the treating medical professional" that states the start date and
expected duration of your incapacity. Your care team can use Service Canada's own form or their own
letterhead, as long as it is readable, names you, carries the practitioner's stamp or signature, and
gives the start date and expected duration.

Who can sign is a longer list than most people expect:

| Can complete and sign your certificate |
| --- |
| Medical doctor · nurse practitioner · registered nurse (in isolated areas when a doctor is unavailable) |
| Chiropractor · podiatrist · optometrist · dentist · midwife |
| Psychologist |

They must be practising in Canada or the United States, and "the illness or injury they're treating
must be in their field of expertise." For Parkinson's that realistically means your neurologist or
family doctor — but if the delay is only about getting an appointment, a nurse practitioner in the same
clinic may be able to sign sooner.

Keep the certificate for **six years**. You may not be asked for it up front, and you may be asked for
it later.

<Callout text="Ask for the certificate to state the <b>expected duration</b> generously and honestly rather than optimistically. A short duration on the form is a date at which your benefit stops and you have to go back for another one — and clinic appointments do not always arrive when you need them." />

The third step is the one that gets skipped, and it is the reason this page exists. Parkinson's disease
is on Service Canada's grave conditions list, which targets a 30-day decision rather than 120 — the
details are in [Canada's federal disability benefits](/en/institutions/canada-disability-benefits/).
Even so, starting the claim early is the difference between the two benefits meeting and the two
benefits missing each other by a season.

One thing CPP disability will not do, whenever it arrives: "CPP disability benefits do not cover
medications, medical devices, or other health costs." That side of the ledger is provincial, and is
covered in [your Parkinson's drug bill depends on your province](/en/institutions/canada-provincial-drug-coverage/).

## After you apply

**Biweekly reports.** You must file a report every two weeks for as long as you are paid, using your
social insurance number and the four-digit access code Service Canada mails you. If you work or earn
anything, it goes on the report — otherwise you risk an overpayment you have to give back.

**Travel.** "You're not usually eligible to receive sickness benefits while outside Canada." There is
an exception if you are "obtaining medical treatment that isn't readily or immediately available in
your area of residence." Tell Service Canada in advance either way.

**If your situation changes.** Contact them if you recover earlier than expected or need more time off
than expected. For a progressive condition the second is the likely one, and it is not automatic.

**If you are refused.** You can request a reconsideration, but "you must submit a request for
reconsideration within 30 days after the day the decision was communicated to you." Thirty days is
short. Diarise it the day the letter arrives.

## EI caregiving benefits usually do not cover Parkinson's

This is the section that saves a care partner a wasted application.

EI has three caregiving benefits:

| Benefit | For | Maximum weeks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Family benefit for children | a critically ill or injured child under 18 | up to 35 weeks |
| Family benefit for adults | a critically ill or injured person 18 or older | up to 15 weeks |
| Compassionate care benefits | someone who requires end-of-life care | up to 26 weeks |

The adult one sounds like the right one. Read Service Canada's definition of a critically ill or
injured person to the end.

<SourceQuote
  quote="A critically ill or injured person is someone whose baseline state of health has changed significantly because of illness or injury. As a result, their life is at risk as a direct or indirect result of illness or injury and care or support is needed from at least 1 caregiver. Their condition must be certified by a medical doctor or nurse practitioner. If the person is already living with a chronic medical condition, caregivers aren't eligible for benefits unless the person's health changes significantly because of a new and acute life-threatening event."
  attribution="Service Canada"
  name="EI caregiving benefits and leave"
  url="https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/ei/caregiving.html"
/>

Parkinson's is a chronic condition, and progression — even fast progression — is not a new and acute
life-threatening event. A care partner who takes unpaid leave expecting 15 weeks of EI is likely to be
refused.

Two situations do change the answer. A **new acute event** — a fall with a serious fracture, aspiration
pneumonia, a hospital admission that changes the baseline — can meet the test, certified by a doctor or
nurse practitioner. And **compassionate care benefits** apply where the person "has a serious medical
condition with a significant risk of death within 26 weeks."

<Callout text="Where these benefits do apply, two rules are more generous than people expect. You do <b>not</b> have to live with the person or be related to them — EI includes anyone considered to be like a family member. And the weeks can be <b>shared</b> between several people, at the same time or one after another." />

If the caregiving benefits do not fit, the support that usually does is on the tax side rather than the
income side — the federal credit for supporting a family member with an impairment, and the transfer of
unused Disability Tax Credit amounts. Those are in
[the tax credits that come after the DTC](/en/institutions/canada-tax-credits/).

## A note for Quebec

Employment Insurance is federal, so EI sickness benefits work the same way in Quebec as everywhere
else — same 26 weeks, same $729, same 600 hours. What differs in Quebec is what comes next: the
disability pension is the **Quebec Pension Plan**, administered by Retraite Québec, not the CPP. See
[living with Parkinson's in Quebec](/en/institutions/canada-quebec/).

## If this applies to you

| Your situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| You have just stopped working and don't know what to claim | Ask your employer about sick leave or short-term disability first, then apply for EI sickness. Applying does not stop you claiming employer benefits |
| Your symptoms are variable — some weeks you could work | The test is a decrease in weekly earnings of more than 40% for at least one week, plus being unable to work for medical reasons. Apply and let Service Canada decide rather than ruling yourself out |
| You had already cut down to part-time before stopping | Check your insured hours. Six hundred hours in 52 weeks is the bar, and a long taper can put you under it |
| You are self-employed | Regular self-employment is not insured. If you registered for EI special benefits for self-employed people, different rules apply — contact Service Canada |
| You are on EI sickness and it is week 12 | Start the CPP disability application now if you have not. A standard decision targets 120 days |
| Your certificate says "8 weeks" and you are not better | Contact Service Canada before it lapses and get an updated certificate. Benefits do not extend on their own |
| You are a care partner planning to take leave | EI caregiving benefits generally exclude chronic conditions. Check whether an acute event has changed the baseline before you rely on them |
| Your EI application was refused | Request a reconsideration within 30 days of the decision being communicated to you |
