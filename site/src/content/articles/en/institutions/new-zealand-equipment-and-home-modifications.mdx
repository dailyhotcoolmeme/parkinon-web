New Zealand funds two different things here and it helps to keep them apart from the start.

**Equipment** is objects that come into the house — a shower stool, a walking frame, an adjustable
bed, a wheelchair. It is loaned to you, not bought for you.

**Home modifications** are changes to the building — a rail, a ramp, a level-access shower, a wider
doorway. That is a construction job with a means test attached.

Both run through **Equipment and Modification Services (EMS)**, and both start with an assessor rather
than with a catalogue. Checked in **August 2026**.

## The assessor is the gatekeeper, and it is usually an occupational therapist

Disability Support Services says "EMS assessors are usually occupational therapists,
physiotherapists, speech-language therapists or other hearing and vision professionals."

You can be referred by your doctor, or refer yourself through your local hospital's community health
services. You do not need to have decided what you want. An assessor's job is to watch you do the
thing that has become difficult and work out what would fix it — which is often not what you had in
mind.

<Callout text="Do the assessment at the <b>hard time of day</b>, in the <b>actual room</b>. An assessor who watches you get out of your own bath at your worst hour will recommend something different from one who talks it through at a table at 11am." />

## Who your provider is depends on where you live

| Region | Provider | Contact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Auckland and Northland | Accessable | 0508 001 002 · info@accessable.co.nz |
| All other regions | Enable New Zealand | 0800 17 1981 · enable@enable.co.nz |

Both do the same job. The split is purely geographic, which matters if you move house or if you are
reading advice written for the other half of the country.

## What can be loaned

DSS lists equipment including "wheelchairs, shower stools, raised toilet seats, adjustable beds,
walking frames, hoists, communication devices" and "visual or vibrating alert systems."

It is a loan. The provider "can repair equipment when it is damaged and can collect it when you no
longer need it." That is better than it sounds — repair and replacement are somebody else's problem,
and as Parkinson's changes, equipment can be swapped rather than bought again.

Two exclusions to know: equipment needed for **less than six months** is outside the scheme, and so is
low-cost equipment, which is where the real frustration lies.

## The $50 rule, and why it stings with Parkinson's

DSS: "Low-cost equipment is any item that costs less than $50 and can support you to do everyday tasks
safely and independently." Generally it will only fund those items for:

- "children under 16 years of age"
- "people who can show that they cannot afford to buy the things themselves, for example, they are
  receiving either a special benefit or temporary additional support from Work and Income"
- "people who are living in residential care, are under 65 years of age and need the equipment because
  it is essential to support their personal safety"

Now read the examples DSS gives of low-cost items: "special cutlery, electric can openers, raisers for
a chair or bed, long-handled hair brushes/combs, reachers, dressing aids, short grab rails, walking
sticks."

<Callout text="That is not a random list. It is almost exactly what a person in the first years of Parkinson's needs — weighted cutlery for tremor, chair raisers for standing up, dressing aids for buttons, a short rail beside the toilet. <b>The cheap things that help most are the ones you are expected to buy yourself.</b>" />

There are two honest routes around it, and neither is a loophole:

- **Disability Allowance** reimburses ongoing disability costs up to $82.85 a week, untaxed, and does
  not require you to be on a benefit. Keep receipts. See
  [Supported Living Payment and Disability Allowance](/en/institutions/new-zealand-supported-living-payment/).
- **Flexible funding**, if you have it, has been used to "purchase accessible kitchen equipment and
  utensils that are easier for him to grip and use safely." But equipment needs **prior approval at
  every tier, at any price** — ask the host before you buy, not after. See
  [Individualised Funding](/en/institutions/new-zealand-individualised-funding/).

## Home modifications: what is funded and what is refused

</figure>

DSS may fund "handrails to help you use a set of steps", "a ramp or lift", "a shower with level
access", "changes to doorways", and "fencing that will help stop you being injured".

It will not fund modifications that:

<Checklist
  items={[
    'cost less than $200',
    'have "already been completed before we have approved funding"',
    'are fittings "that you would find in a standard home"',
    'are for general maintenance',
    'are "short-term modifications that might not support you to live in your home for at least 2 to 3 years"',
  ]}
/>

<Callout text="The second and the fifth are the two that cost people money. <b>Work already done is not funded, ever</b> — approval first, builder second. And a modification has to still be doing its job in two to three years, which with a progressive condition means arguing for what you will need <b>then</b>, not only what you need this month." />

That last point is worth pushing on with an assessor. A rail that suits you today may be irrelevant
once walking has changed. A level-access shower usually still works in five years. When the assessor
asks what you need, describe the direction things are moving, not just the current position.

## The money ladder

| Threshold | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Under $200 | Not funded |
| Over $1,000 excluding GST | DSS "will make a cost contribution rather than pay the full cost" if one of the specified circumstances applies |
| Over $8,076 including GST | "You must complete an income and cash asset test if the modifications will cost more than $8,076 (including GST)" |
| Decline the test | "You can only get up to $8,076 (including GST)" |
| Access modifications | Funding for access into or between levels of your home — for example a ramp or platform lift — is limited to $15,334 including GST |

The test is run by Work and Income on DSS's behalf: "Work and Income, on behalf of DSS, will work out
the amount you need to pay, if any, towards the modifications to your home."

Who is counted in the test is broader than you might expect. It looks at the income and assets of
your partner, "your parents or guardians (if you are 16 years of age or older)", and "the owners of
the property". Cash assets means "bank deposits, shares or any other investments or property you
own" — but "you do not need to include the home you are living in as an asset."

And there is a cash-flow trap in the payment order: where a cost contribution applies, DSS's share is
paid after "you have paid the total cost of the work." Ask your assessor exactly how the payment will
run in your case before the builder starts, because "we will contribute" and "we will pay you back"
are very different things for a household on a fixed income.

## What to ask for, if you have Parkinson's

None of this is medical advice — the assessor decides. But these are the situations that come up
often enough to be worth naming out loud so they are not missed:

<Checklist
  items={[
    'Doorways and narrow thresholds, if you find yourself stopping or getting stuck in them',
    'A level-access shower rather than a step over a bath edge — a step is where a lot of falls happen',
    'A rail at the point where you turn, not only along the straight run. Turning is harder than walking',
    'Getting up: chair and bed height, and whether raisers or an adjustable bed would do it',
    'Getting to the toilet at night — lighting on the route, and a rail at the toilet itself',
    'Whether an assessment is worth repeating in a year or two. Needs change, and a new assessment is the way the allocation changes',
  ]}
/>

## If this applies to you

| Your situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| You do not know who your provider is | Auckland or Northland, ring Accessable on 0508 001 002. Anywhere else, Enable New Zealand on 0800 17 1981 |
| You need a shower stool or a walking frame | Ask your GP for an EMS referral, or self-refer through your local hospital's community health services |
| You need cutlery, a reacher, chair raisers or a walking stick | These are low-cost items and generally not funded. Claim them through Disability Allowance instead |
| You have flexible funding and want to buy a gadget | Equipment needs prior approval at every tier. Ask the host first |
| A builder has quoted you for a ramp | Do not start. Work "already been completed before we have approved funding" is not funded |
| The job will cost more than $8,076 including GST | You will need to complete the income and cash asset test. Declining it caps you at $8,076 |
| You rent | The property owner's position is part of the test and their consent will matter. Raise it with the assessor early |
| Your assessment was two years ago and things have changed | Ask for a reassessment. Equipment can be swapped as needs change |
