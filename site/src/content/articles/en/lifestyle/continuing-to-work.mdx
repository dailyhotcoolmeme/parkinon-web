A Parkinson's diagnosis is not a reason to stop working. Early on, symptoms often don't interfere
much with the job at all. In one study of 88 people diagnosed at age 65 or younger, <b>40% were
still employed five years on</b>, and 14% at ten years, with a median time to leaving work of
<b>seven years</b>. That is a long runway — and what you put in place during it makes a real
difference to how long you keep working.

## When to tell people is up to you

There is <b>no legal obligation</b> to tell your employer about a diagnosis. It is your information.

That said, timing has consequences. If you wait until symptoms have already started affecting your
performance, you end up having two conversations at once — the diagnosis and the drop in output —
and it becomes much harder to explain why you need adjustments. Telling people before the problem
becomes visible, at a point when you feel ready, is generally what specialists recommend. As one
person quoted by the American Parkinson Disease Association put it: being proactive means you get to
control the narrative and decide how much to share.

And you do decide how much. You don't have to give a full account of the condition — just the parts
that affect your work, in the detail the situation actually needs. If people want to understand more,
point them at a reputable organization's website rather than turning yourself into the explainer.

## You can ask for adjustments at work

In the United States, the <b>Americans with Disabilities Act</b> protects people with Parkinson's
from discrimination in hiring, on the job and in termination, and requires employers with 15 or more
employees to provide <b>reasonable accommodations</b> — unless doing so would impose an undue
hardship on the business. Many other countries have comparable duties under different names, so
check what applies where you work; the principle is usually similar even when the law isn't.

Adjustments that are reported to help in practice include:

- An <b>ergonomic workstation</b> and adaptive computer equipment — a trackball instead of a mouse,
  for example
- <b>Voice recognition software</b>, for when handwriting or typing gets harder
- A <b>speech amplifier</b>, if your voice has got quieter
- Permission to use a cane, walker or mobility scooter to get around the site
- <b>Flexible hours, part-time work, or working from home</b>
- A quieter workspace with fewer interruptions, plus written instructions and memory aids
- <b>Scheduled rest breaks</b>

Ask specifically. A request that names the adjustment, and says what it would let you do, is far
more likely to be granted than a general statement that things have got harder. It also helps to
know that plenty of these cost the employer almost nothing — a schedule change, written
instructions, a headset, a different mouse.

## There is free expert help on what to ask for

You don't have to work out the request on your own. In the United States, the <b>Job Accommodation
Network (JAN)</b> is a free consulting service funded by the U.S. Department of Labor that advises on
job accommodations, the ADA and the employability of people with disabilities. It is reachable at
<b>1-800-526-7234</b> or [askjan.org](https://askjan.org/), and it publishes a page specifically on
Parkinson's along with a searchable database of accommodation options.

Two things worth knowing about JAN. First, it advises <b>employers</b> too — so if your manager or
HR contact is unsure what is reasonable, they can call as well, which takes the argument out of the
room. Second, its guidance is explicit that people with Parkinson's rarely develop all the possible
limitations, and many need only a few adjustments or none at all. Your request does not have to be
comprehensive to be legitimate.

## Reduced hours and leave are worth asking about

Sick leave and extended leave are largely governed by your employer's own policies, so the first
step is finding out from HR what actually exists — paid sick leave, short-term and long-term
disability coverage, and whether any statutory leave applies to you.

In the United States, the <b>Family and Medical Leave Act</b> provides eligible employees at covered
employers up to <b>12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave</b>. Other countries have their own
schemes with different eligibility and pay. Beyond formal leave, smaller changes often do more work
than people expect: shifting your hours to match when you feel best, or staggered start and finish
times to avoid the worst of a commute.

## Where to get help

If it is hard to judge on your own, a vocational rehabilitation counselor or a social worker at your
clinic can go through which supports you are actually eligible for where you live. In the U.S., the
Parkinson's Foundation Helpline (<b>1-800-4PD-INFO</b>, 1-800-473-4636) is a good first call, and JAN
is the specialist for the workplace side.

## What you can do now

If the job is still manageable, there is no rush. But think through in advance <b>when, who and how
much</b> you would tell — and read up on the adjustments above — so that if symptoms do progress, it
doesn't arrive as a crisis.
