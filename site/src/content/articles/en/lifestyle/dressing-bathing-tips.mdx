Things you have always taken for granted, like getting dressed and washing, can start to take a
long time or become risky as Parkinson's progresses. Changing just a few things makes them much
easier.

## When and how you dress matters

Get dressed <b>while your medication is working at its best</b>. Movement is easier once the
medication has taken effect than right after you swallow your morning dose. Rather than perching
on the edge of the bed, <b>sit in a sturdy chair with armrests</b> to steady yourself and get
ready — it is safer — and a gentle stretch before you start leaves your muscles less stiff. When
you put clothes on, work the <b>more affected or stiffer arm or leg into the sleeve or trouser leg
first</b>; it is far more comfortable that way. Choosing what to wear each morning also takes
energy and time — hanging up a few days' outfits in advance, or simplifying your combinations,
noticeably shortens the morning routine.

## Try changing fabrics and fasteners

Small buttons are especially hard when your hands tremor or fine finger movement is difficult.
Switching to <b>velcro or magnetic fasteners</b> makes dressing and undressing much easier, and
you can have them fitted to clothes you already own. For waistbands, <b>elastic or stretch
bands</b> are more comfortable than a belt, and it is best to avoid high-friction fabrics like
velour, which drag against the skin. For socks, look for non-slip socks without a tight elastic
band; for shoes, lightweight ones with velcro or elastic laces.

## Safety comes first when you wash

A tub or shower should have <b>at least two professionally installed grab bars</b>. Towel rails,
soap dishes and faucets must never be used as grab bars — they cannot take your weight and can
break off. Put a slip-resistant mat on the floor, and use <b>liquid soap</b> rather than a bar,
which is less likely to slip out of your hands. Turning the water heater down to <b>120°F (49°C)
or lower</b> is a safe way to prevent scalds.

## Sit down to wash your face, brush your teeth and shave

Brushing your teeth, shaving and doing your hair are all <b>better done sitting than standing</b>
— it prevents falls and saves your energy. Resting your elbows on the sink also helps if your arms
or shoulders tire easily. Using an <b>electric razor</b> instead of a blade greatly reduces the
risk of cuts when your hands are shaky. If you wear makeup, <b>pump bottles</b> are easier to
squeeze than tubes, and a <b>brush with a thick handle</b> is easier to grip than a thin one. Some
people find that listening to music with a clear rhythm while brushing their teeth makes the
movement easier, because they can move to the beat.

## Equipment makes it much easier

If standing on your own feels unsteady, wash sitting on a <b>tub transfer bench or shower
chair</b>, and use a handheld showerhead so you have to change position less. A shelf mounted
between shoulder and knee height lets you reach shampoo and soap without bending. Smaller aids
help too — a buttonhook, a zipper pull, a sock aid. An occupational therapist can give you
specific recommendations about which ones suit you.

## If you're a care partner helping out

The most important thing when helping someone dress is <b>allowing plenty of time</b>. Rushing
makes symptoms worse through stress, and often ends with the person unable to do even the parts
they could have managed themselves. Choosing clothes in <b>shapes that are easy to help with</b>
— front-opening tops, underwear that fastens at the front — also reduces the load on you. When
you help, give short, simple instructions one at a time ("this arm first"); they are easier to
follow than several movements explained at once.

## Be ready just in case

If you fall in the bathroom it can be hard to call for help, so keep a phone or an emergency call
device within reach. If washing alone worries you, one option is to choose a time when someone
else is nearby.
