About 60% of people with Parkinson's are reported to fall at least once a year, and a substantial
share of them fall repeatedly. What matters most is that this mostly happens not outdoors but
<b>inside the familiar home</b>. Making small changes room by room can cut the risk substantially.

  <img src={armchairImg.src} alt="An armchair in a neatly arranged living room" />
</figure>

## The bathroom — the most dangerous room

The bathroom is where falls happen most. Fit professionally installed grab bars around the toilet,
tub and shower, and if you need them, add a raised toilet seat or a safe bench you can sit on
inside the tub. Keep the floor slip-resistant, and it is best to take small rugs, which are easy to
trip on, out of the bathroom altogether.

## Living room and hallways — clear the trip hazards first

Clearing clutter off the floor and removing unsecured rugs is the basis of fall prevention.
Arrange furniture so it doesn't block walking routes, and if you use a walker or a cane, make sure
the routes are wide enough. Lighting the whole home brightly enough to reduce dark or shadowed
corners matters too.

## The bedroom — pay particular attention to night-time

Adjust the bed to a height where your feet touch the floor when you sit on it, and keep a light
switch and a phone within reach beside the bed. Leaving a <b>night light</b> on along the route to
the bathroom prevents falls from moving around in the dark. If turning over in bed is hard, the bed
rails covered in
[Turning over and getting out of bed](/en/lifestyle/bed-mobility-tips/) can help too.

## Stairs — handrails on both sides, and mark the top and bottom

Ideally stairs have <b>handrails on both sides</b>, mounted about 2 to 3 inches (5 to 7 cm) out
from the wall so you can get a solid grip. Marking the point where the stairs start and end with
bright tape gives you a visible reference point, which helps steady your steps in the way
[When your feet freeze mid-step](/en/lifestyle/freezing-of-gait-tips/) describes. It is also worth
checking whether the stair surface has a slip-resistant finish.

## The kitchen — keep what you use often down low

Store the dishes and cooking tools you use most in <b>low drawers you can reach easily</b> rather
than on high shelves. Swapping round knobs for bar-style handles, which are easier to grip, makes
doors and drawers easier to open when fine finger movement is difficult. If you have to get
something down from up high, don't strain onto your toes — use a long-handled reacher or ask
someone else.

## Check the thresholds between rooms too

The <b>door sills</b> your feet catch on as you move from room to room cause falls more often than
you would expect. Where possible, remove them or replace them with a low ramp.

## Pets and cords are easy-to-miss trip hazards

A dog or cat suddenly cutting under your feet, or a food bowl or toy left in a walkway, is a
frequently overlooked cause of falls. Keeping the spots where pets usually settle separate from
walking routes helps. If electrical cords run across the floor, route them along the wall or tape
them down so you don't trip on them.

## Walk through each room with someone else

The more time you spend in a home, the harder it is to spot the risky parts yourself. If someone
close to you takes this room-by-room list and walks through the house, they often notice trip
hazards and dark corners you had long since stopped seeing. There is no need to fix everything at
once — starting with whatever looks most dangerous and working through one at a time is enough.

## Be ready just in case

Keep a phone or an emergency call device close to you at all times so you can call for help right
away if you fall. If you don't know where to start, you can ask your care team for a <b>home
safety assessment</b>, in which an occupational therapist looks at your home directly and helps
you set priorities.

> This article is not a substitute for medical diagnosis or treatment. If falls are frequent or
> worrying, please talk with your care team.
