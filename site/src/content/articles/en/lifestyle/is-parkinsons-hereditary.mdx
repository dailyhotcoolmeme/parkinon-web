A Parkinson's diagnosis tends to bring the worry along with it: "does this mean my children or my
siblings will get it too?" The short answer is that <b>in most cases the chance of it being passed
on is not high enough to worry about.</b>

## Most of it is not inherited

For <b>about 90%</b> of people with Parkinson's, the condition is <b>idiopathic</b> — no gene
variant known so far is involved. Only the remaining 10% or so is understood to be linked to
specific gene variants such as LRRK2, GBA or SNCA (for a fuller explanation of the genes involved,
see [why Parkinson's happens](/en/lifestyle/why-parkinsons-happens/)).

## How much does a family history raise the risk?

The lifetime risk of developing Parkinson's for the general population is about <b>1%</b>. If a
close relative such as a parent or sibling has Parkinson's, that risk is reported to rise to around
<b>2%</b>. It is true that this doubles the risk, but in absolute terms it is still low — having a
family history does not mean you will definitely develop Parkinson's.

## Carrying the gene does not mean you will develop it

Genes like LRRK2 can be passed on with just one copy of the variant (<Term brief="A pattern of inheritance in which the condition can develop even if the variant gene comes from only one parent. LRRK2 and others are inherited this way." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-are-autosomal-dominant-and-recessive-inheritance">autosomal dominant</Term>), but inheriting the variant does not mean a 100% chance of developing the condition. This is called <Term brief="It means that inheriting a particular gene variant does not necessarily mean the condition will develop. Among family members carrying the same variant, some develop symptoms and others never do in their lifetime." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-is-incomplete-penetrance">incomplete penetrance</Term>. For example, the probability that someone with an LRRK2 variant actually experiences Parkinson's symptoms varies with age: it is reported at about 28% by age 59, about 51% by 69, and about 74% by 79 — meaning the probability rises as you get older, but a fair number of people go their whole lives without symptoms. Genes like <Term brief="The gene that makes the parkin protein. It is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern and is known as one of the common genetic causes of early-onset (before age 50) Parkinson's disease." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-is-prkn">PRKN</Term>, by contrast, follow an <Term brief="A pattern of inheritance in which the variant gene must be inherited from both parents for the condition to develop. PRKN and others are associated with this pattern, and it is linked to early-onset Parkinson's disease." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-are-autosomal-dominant-and-recessive-inheritance">autosomal recessive</Term> pattern, requiring the variant from both parents, and are associated with early-onset Parkinson's diagnosed at a relatively young age.

## The rates differ by ancestry and region

How often LRRK2- and GBA-related variants appear varies widely between populations. In
<b>Ashkenazi Jewish</b> and <b>North African Berber</b> populations, for instance, a particular
LRRK2 variant is reported to be common enough to be found in roughly 14% and 30% to 40%
respectively of everyone with Parkinson's — while in other populations it is far rarer. Differences
like these also mean that the significance of a genetic test result can change depending on your
own background.

## Do you need a genetic test?

For most people, genetic testing isn't necessary. It is worth considering in cases like these.

- Several people in the family have Parkinson's
- You were diagnosed at a relatively young age
- You need to check eligibility for a clinical trial that targets a specific gene

Direct-to-consumer genetic test kits check only a limited set of variants, so their results can
mislead. If you are considering testing, it is better to talk with a <b>genetic counselor</b>
before and after, so you understand accurately what the result actually means.

## What you can do now

If family history worries you, getting accurate risk information from your care team or a genetic
counselor beats worrying vaguely about it. Having a gene variant does not mean you will definitely
develop the condition, and conversely Parkinson's can develop without one — genetics is only one
cause among several.
