When people hear that Parkinson's affects thinking, they usually picture memory getting worse. In
practice it tends to have a bigger effect on <b>holding your attention and working through several
things in order (executive function)</b>. Rather than pure memory loss, it more often feels like
"I can't concentrate, and doing several things at once has got harder."

## It isn't that nothing gets stored — it's hard to pull back out

The memory problems in Parkinson's are different in character from dementia such as Alzheimer's
disease. It isn't that the information fails to get stored; it is that <b>retrieving</b> stored
information at the moment you need it is often difficult. So a vague question like "what was that
thing, don't you remember?" may get no answer, while a hint or a set of options — "was it fish, or
vegetables?" — often makes it far easier to recall. Family simply asking in that second way can
make real conversations much easier.

## What mild cognitive impairment is

<Term brief="A state in which thinking has slowed a little compared with others of the same age, but daily life can still be managed independently. It is reported in 20% to 50% of people with Parkinson's." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-is-mild-cognitive-impairment-mci">Mild cognitive impairment (MCI)</Term> means thinking has slowed somewhat relative to others of the same age, but daily life can still be handled independently. It is reported in 20% to 50% of people with Parkinson's. Not all mild cognitive impairment progresses to dementia, and noticing it early and managing it is known to slow the decline, and in some cases reverse part of it.

## What helps

- **Get enough sleep.** Giving yourself at least 6 hours a night, and ideally around 8, helps
  attention and executive function.
- **Focus on one thing at a time.** Instead of trying to handle several things at once, cut down
  on distractions and work through them one by one — it is more efficient.
- **Keep up strength training.** Resistance exercise has been studied not only for balance and
  walking but also for improving executive function and attention.
- **Keep using your brain.** Reading, puzzles, memory games and learning something new all help.
- **Lean on outside tools like calendars, reminders and notes.** Handing things over to something
  you can see beats straining to hold it all in your head.

## Be especially careful with safety-critical activities like driving

If your attention or the speed of your judgment doesn't feel the way it used to, activities that
demand split-second reactions — driving above all — need particularly careful thought. You may
feel fine while your actual reaction time has changed, so asking your family what they see, or
getting your driving ability assessed if needed, is one way to handle it.

## What you can do now

If you notice changes in your thinking, it is better to tell your care team than to judge it on
your own. Cognitive rehabilitation or medications such as rivastigmine may help where they are
needed, and above all it matters to start managing it early.

> This article is not a substitute for medical diagnosis or treatment. If changes in your memory
> or concentration worry you, please talk with your care team.
