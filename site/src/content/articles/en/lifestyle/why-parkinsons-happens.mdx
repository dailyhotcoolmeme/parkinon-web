When you are diagnosed with Parkinson's disease, the question "why me?" follows naturally. What
research has shown so far is that in most cases it isn't down to a single cause, but to several
factors overlapping.

## In most cases there is no clear cause

In 85% to 90% of people with Parkinson's, the condition is <b>idiopathic</b> — meaning no clear
cause can be identified. It can develop with no one else in the family affected, and it is not a
condition you bring on by doing something wrong.

## Genetics accounts for about 10% to 15%

The remaining 10% to 15% is known to involve specific genes. Variants in genes such as <Term brief="The leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 gene. Variants in it are linked to Parkinson's that starts at a relatively later age." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-are-lrrk2-gba-and-snca">LRRK2</Term>, <Term brief="Variants in this gene raise the risk of Parkinson's and tend to come with cognitive decline that progresses relatively faster." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-are-lrrk2-gba-and-snca">GBA</Term> and <Term brief="The gene that makes the alpha-synuclein protein. Variants in it are linked to Parkinson's that starts at a relatively younger age." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-are-lrrk2-gba-and-snca">SNCA</Term> are being studied as factors that raise the risk of Parkinson's. Carrying one of these variants does not mean you will definitely develop Parkinson's, and genetic factors weigh more heavily when there is a family history or when the diagnosis comes at a relatively young age. If family history is on your mind, our article on [whether Parkinson's is hereditary](/en/lifestyle/is-parkinsons-hereditary/) covers it in more detail.

## Age and environmental factors also play a part

<b>Age</b> is the biggest risk factor, because dopamine nerve cells naturally decline as you get older. Among environmental factors, <b>pesticides</b> have been studied the most — in particular, one study found that long exposure to a herbicide called <Term brief="A herbicide used in agriculture. Studies have reported that long-term exposure raises the risk of Parkinson's." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-are-paraquat-and-trichloroethylene">paraquat</Term> raised the risk of Parkinson's by up to 150%. Industrial solvents such as <Term brief="An industrial solvent used in dry cleaning and metal degreasing. It is one of the most common groundwater contaminants, and studies link long-term exposure to a higher risk of Parkinson's." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-are-paraquat-and-trichloroethylene">trichloroethylene (TCE)</Term>, used in dry cleaning and metal degreasing, have also been reported to raise the risk with long-term exposure through groundwater, and air pollution is another environmental factor now under study. There is also research suggesting that Parkinson's is more likely to appear in people who are genetically at risk when they are exposed to these environmental factors — in other words, genes and environment don't act separately, but influence each other.

## Some studies find it is less common in smokers and coffee drinkers

Several observational studies have found a <b>lower risk of Parkinson's among smokers and among
people who regularly consume caffeine.</b> This is not a result you can read as "smoking or
caffeine prevents Parkinson's" — smoking clearly raises other serious risks such as
cardiovascular disease and cancer, so researchers think the correlation is driven not by nicotine
or caffeine themselves but by some other component, or by a cause not yet identified, and they
are still looking into it.

## The hypothesis that the gut, not just the brain, is involved

The <Term brief="A hypothesis that in some people alpha-synuclein starts clumping in the gut or the olfactory nerve before it does in the brain, then slowly travels up to the brain along the vagus nerve." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-are-the-braak-hypothesis-and-the-vagus-nerve">Braak hypothesis</Term>, which has drawn attention relatively recently, proposes that in some people the <b>alpha-synuclein</b> protein starts clumping in the nerves of the gut or in the olfactory nerve before it does in the brain, and then slowly travels up to the brain along the <Term brief="The nerve that connects the brain to internal organs including the gut. In the Braak hypothesis it is identified as the route alpha-synuclein takes from the gut to the brain." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-are-the-braak-hypothesis-and-the-vagus-nerve">vagus nerve</Term>. If the hypothesis holds, it could help explain why non-motor symptoms such as constipation and a reduced sense of smell so often show up before motor symptoms like hand tremor. Research continues to find that the makeup of gut bacteria differs in people with Parkinson's, but this is not a settled conclusion yet.

## What is certain, and what is still unknown

The process by which dopamine nerve cells are lost in the brain is known to involve a protein called <Term brief="A protein normally present in the brain. In Parkinson's, it is known to clump abnormally and build up inside nerve cells." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-is-alpha-synuclein">alpha-synuclein</Term> clumping abnormally. Why this protein starts clumping, and why in some people and not others, is still not fully understood. Research to uncover the cause continues around the world today.
