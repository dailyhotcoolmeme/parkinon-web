This issue brings together two studies: a <b>new way to deliver treatment inside cells</b>, and
how a <b>gene that determines skin and hair color</b> connects to how fast Parkinson's progresses.
One is a delivery-technology study; the other is a large cohort study of a common gene variant and
progression speed.

<StoryHead index={1} first />

## Antibodies that could not reach the inside of cells were delivered there using nanoparticles

### 🔍 How this came about

Researchers at Tel Aviv University, Cornell University, and the Technion — Israel Institute of
Technology developed a
<Term brief="A tiny carrier made of a fat-like shell that transports drugs into cells." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-is-a-lipid-nanoparticle-lnp">lipid
nanoparticle (LNP)</Term> technology for delivering antibodies inside cells. Antibodies are
powerful at targeting molecules on the cell surface or outside the cell, but they cannot cross the
cell membrane, so they have largely been unusable against targets located inside cells — even
though a large share of disease-related proteins sit inside cells.

The team solved this by temporarily <b>wrapping antibodies with a synthetic molecule called
SL4</b>. The masked antibodies loaded far more efficiently into nanoparticles, and once inside the
cell they regained their original structure and function. Applying this to an antibody targeting
<Term brief="A protein normally present in the brain. In Parkinson's it clumps abnormally and builds up inside dopamine neurons." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-is-alpha-synuclein">alpha-synuclein</Term>
and giving it systemically in a Parkinson's animal model <b>significantly reduced pathological
alpha-synuclein clumps</b>. The same approach, applied to an inflammation-related antibody in an
acute lung injury model, reduced inflammation markers and improved lung tissue.

<SourceQuote quote="This approach efficiently delivers various therapeutic antibodies into multiple cancer cell lines, inhibiting key transcription factors in inflammatory and cancer signaling pathways. We further demonstrate systemic delivery of therapeutic antibodies in disease models, including alpha-synuclein-specific antibodies for Parkinson's disease and RelA-specific immunoglobulins for acute lung injury." attribution="Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences abstract" name="Read the original" url="https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2531649123" />

### ✅ If you live with Parkinson's

This is not a treatment available for people — it is <b>animal-model research validating the
delivery technology itself</b>. Antibody therapies are already widely used for cancer and
autoimmune disease, but treatments aimed at targets inside cells need a delivery technology like
this one before they can move into human trials.

<Callout text="Whether this cell-delivery technology is safe and effective in people has not yet been established. This news alone does not mean a new treatment is imminent." />

### 🙋 If you are a care partner

When you come across a phrase like "a treatment targeting alpha-synuclein," it helps to check
which stage it is at. Animal-model research, early human trials, and large clinical trials are
different stages, and this research is currently at the earliest of those.

## <Term brief="Melanocortin 1 receptor, a gene best known for determining skin and hair pigmentation. This study found it also helps regulate oxidative stress in substantia nigra neurons." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-is-mc1r">MC1R</Term> variant carriers showed up to 63% faster motor decline

### 🔍 How this came about

Researchers at Massachusetts General Hospital (Harvard Medical School) analyzed data from 809
participants in the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) and a separate group of 587
clinical trial participants — 1,396 people in total — to examine how a <b>loss-of-function MC1R
variant</b> relates to the speed of motor decline. MC1R is best known as the gene that determines
skin and hair pigmentation, and this variant is carried by <b>more than 60% of people of European
descent</b>.

The analysis found that MC1R variant carriers showed <b>30% faster motor decline</b> on the
<Term brief="A scale used to assess motor and non-motor symptoms of Parkinson's and their effect on daily life." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-is-the-updrs">UPDRS</Term>
motor score than non-carriers, and in a separate replication cohort that gap widened to
<b>50%</b>. Among carriers who inherited the variant from both parents, progression was up to
<b>63%</b> faster. Even among participants not yet diagnosed with Parkinson's, carriers were more
than four times as likely to go on to develop the disease.

<SourceQuote quote="Among 383 participants with sporadic PD, MC1R loss-of-function carriers exhibited 30% faster motor decline than noncarriers (P = .006). In the replication cohort, MC1R loss-of-function carriers exhibited 50% faster motor decline than noncarriers (P = .01)." attribution="JAMA Neurology abstract" name="Read the original" url="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42709445/" />

### ✅ If you live with Parkinson's

The MC1R variant is common and carried by a majority of people of European descent. The
researchers say it could be used to identify a large subgroup of faster-progressing people with
Parkinson's through a <b>simple genetic test</b>, and could become a target for future drug
development. That said, this research was conducted mainly in populations of European descent, and
whether the same proportions apply here has not been confirmed.

<Callout text="Carrying this variant is not a confirmed diagnosis that someone will definitely decline faster. It reflects a statistical difference between groups, and it is not evidence that you need this genetic test right now." />

### 🙋 If you are a care partner

If a genetic test result comes up, talk with your care team about what it means and what, if
anything, should change. The test used in this research is not yet a routine clinical test, and
interpreting results requires considering factors such as population background and interactions
with other genes.

## What you can do now

These two studies sit at different stages — one tested a delivery technology in an animal model,
and the other confirmed how an already-existing gene variant plays out in real people. What you
can do right now is not to seek out a new test on your own, but to <b>keep track of how symptoms
are changing</b> and share that with your care team at the next visit.
