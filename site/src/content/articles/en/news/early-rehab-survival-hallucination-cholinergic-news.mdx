This time both stories are about <b>when to start</b>. One is a large follow-up study in which the
people who began rehabilitation soon after diagnosis lived longer. The other found that changes
were already under way in the brain years before hallucinations actually appeared. Between them,
they give reason to rethink the familiar approach of responding once a symptom has got worse.

<StoryHead index={1} first />

## People who started rehabilitation within a year of diagnosis lived longer

### 🔍 How this came about

A study published in the journal npj Parkinson's Disease followed <b>a nationwide cohort of 19,153
people with Parkinson's in South Korea</b> for up to 15 years, drawing on national health
insurance records. The researchers sorted them into three groups by when rehabilitation began —
within a year of diagnosis (32.2%), between one and two years (13.5%), and more than two years
later (54.3%).

Comparing deaths across the follow-up period, <b>the group that started within the first year
lived longest.</b> Measured against that group, the risk of death was <b>28% higher</b> in those
who started one to two years later, and <b>44% higher</b> in those who started more than two years
later.

The part most worth noting is elsewhere, though. This gap <b>was not confined to people whose
function had clearly declined — it showed up just the same in those who had no difficulty with
daily life yet.</b> The researchers pointed out that care as it is currently delivered tends to be
reactive, with rehabilitation starting only after function has worsened, and argued that it should
instead be part of the baseline care offered at diagnosis.

### ✅ For you

If you were diagnosed recently, it is easy to put rehabilitation off — walking is fine, so it can
come later. What this study points to is exactly the opposite: <b>the people who started before
things became difficult did better.</b> Asking your care team at your next appointment whether now
is a good time to start is enough to get it moving.

<Callout text="This study did not divide people into two groups and test them; it looked back over care records that had already accumulated, which makes it an observational study. So it cannot establish that rehabilitation extended life — it remains possible that people in better health to begin with were the ones who started rehabilitation early. That said, the fact that the same gap appeared in people with no loss of function is hard to explain away on those grounds alone." />

### 🙋 For your care partner

Rehabilitation carries an impression of being something for people who have already declined a
long way, so in the early years it is often turned down even when suggested. This study is
grounds for turning that impression around.

Rehabilitation in Parkinson's is not one thing but <b>three</b>, and each deals with different
problems. Which one to begin with is worth settling together with the care team.

<div class="table-scroll">

| Type | What it mainly deals with |
|---|---|
| Physical therapy | Walking, balance and posture, preventing falls, practising large movements |
| Occupational therapy | Everyday actions such as dressing, eating and handwriting, and adapting the home |
| Speech therapy | A voice growing quieter, articulation, and swallowing (choking) |

</div>

There is no need to start all three at once. Starting with the one that is most troublesome right
now is fine — the point of this study is less about choosing the right type than about <b>not
putting off the start</b>.

## Years before hallucinations appeared, the brain had already begun to change

### 🔍 How this came about

Researchers at Washington University in St. Louis, in the US, published a long-term study in npj
Parkinson's Disease in which the brains of 95 people with Parkinson's were observed using a
<Term brief="A scan that looks not at the shape of the brain but at how it is working. A substance that sticks only to whatever is being looked for is given a very weak radioactive label and injected, and the scanner images where in the brain it collects." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-is-a-pet-scan">PET scan</Term>.
Using a tracer that shows the activity of
<Term brief="One of the substances that carry signals in the brain and nerves. Parkinson's is known as a shortage of dopamine, but the circuits that run on this substance weaken alongside it, and they are involved in memory, attention, visual processing and balance while walking." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-is-acetylcholine">acetylcholine</Term>,
they measured activity in the parts of the brain that process visual information — the thalamus,
the occipital lobe and the temporal lobe among them.

In people who had
<Term brief="The umbrella term for hallucinations (seeing or hearing things that aren't there) and delusions in Parkinson's. Parkinson's medications are frequently the cause, and dementia or delirium can be too. Visual hallucinations are reported in 20–30% of people with Parkinson's." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-is-parkinsons-disease-psychosis">visual hallucinations</Term>,
acetylcholine activity in these areas was <b>clearly lower</b> than in those who did not, and the
lower it was, the more severe the hallucinations. And there is one more finding that matters most
— among those who had no hallucinations at the outset, <b>the ones who went on to develop them
two to six years later already had lower activity before any symptom appeared.</b>

The result held after accounting for age, sex, how long someone had lived with Parkinson's,
thinking ability and whether dementia was present. The researchers said the measurement could
serve as <b>a way of gauging in advance whether hallucinations are likely to develop</b>, or
become a target for treatment aimed at those areas of the brain.

### ✅ For you

Seeing things that aren't there is the kind of symptom people feel too embarrassed to mention, and
so keep to themselves. But as this study shows, <b>it is not something that happens because
someone is fragile — it is a change taking place in the brain.</b> Parkinson's medication is
sometimes the cause as well, which means that saying so opens up room to adjust it. The longer it
stays hidden, the later anything can be done about it.

<Callout text="This is a study of 95 people, and the PET scan used here is a research scan, not a test you can ask for at an appointment. Being able to know in advance is a possibility still at the research stage, and having no hallucinations now does not mean they will never come, nor — the other way round — that they are about to." />

### 🙋 For your care partner

When hallucinations happen for the first time, it is usually the family who are more alarmed, and
the instinct is to insist firmly that there is nothing there. After that, it stops being
mentioned. <b>Listening without alarm, then writing it down and passing it on at the
appointment</b>, is what helps most. It is a symptom that often improves with a change of
medication.

Four things are enough when writing it down.

<ul class="cell-list">
  <li><b>When</b> — the time of day, and in particular whether it clusters around dusk or at night</li>
  <li><b>What</b> — whether it appeared as a person, an animal or an object</li>
  <li><b>How it was taken at the time</b> — whether it was recognised as not being real, or believed to be real. This distinction carries a good deal of weight at the appointment</li>
  <li><b>Whether medication changed around then</b> — note any dose adjustment or newly added medication alongside it</li>
</ul>

Hallucinations do not mean that dementia is around the corner. Left alone, though, they can grow
more frequent, so flagging them early and adjusting medication tends to work out better.

## What you can do now

Both stories point to <b>before</b> rather than <b>after</b> things get worse. If you are in the
early years after diagnosis, ask at your next appointment when rehabilitation should start; if
there is a symptom as hard to raise as hallucinations, write it down and pass it on as it
happened. Both are things that can be started today.
