This time both stories are about timing — <b>how often you take something, and what may be coming
later</b>. One is a levodopa product, newly approved in the European Union, that cut daily doses
from five to three while lengthening the time symptoms stayed controlled. The other is a study
that read gene activity in a blood sample and connected it to what happened years afterwards.

<StoryHead index={1} first />

## Five doses a day down to three — an extended-release levodopa has been approved in the EU

### 🔍 How this came about

The European Commission has approved <b>Hopledo</b>, a levodopa/carbidopa product already sold in
the United States under the name <b>Crexont</b> (development name IPX203). It is for adults who
still have
<Term brief="The cycle between 'on' periods, when medication is controlling symptoms well, and 'off' periods, when the benefit fades and symptoms return. Early on it shows up as predictable wearing off before the next dose; later it can arrive suddenly and irregularly." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-are-dyskinesia-and-motor-fluctuations-on-off">motor fluctuations</Term>
despite treatment.

What is new here is not the ingredient but the <b>build</b>. A single capsule holds both
fast-dissolving immediate-release granules and slow-dissolving
<Term brief="A formulation built to let the medication out slowly rather than all at once. It lasts longer but takes longer to start working. Splitting or chewing it breaks that structure and releases the whole dose at once, so it must never be cut on your own." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-is-an-extended-release-formulation">extended-release</Term>
beads. That is what lets it start working quickly and still last.

The numbers behind the approval come from the
<Term brief="The stage that gathers more safety and efficacy information by comparing across varied groups and doses. Enrolment is large, and this stage generally has to be cleared before a regulator will review the product for approval." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-do-phase-1-2-and-3-mean-in-clinical-trials">phase 3</Term>
RISE-PD trial (630 participants). Compared with immediate-release levodopa/carbidopa, <b>good on
time went up and off time went down.</b> And the number of doses fell rather than rose — an
average of <b>three a day</b> on Hopledo against <b>five</b> on the immediate-release form. A
nine-month extension study found both the symptom control and the dosing frequency held.

Zambon, which holds the rights to sell it in Europe, says it will begin rolling the product out
across European markets <b>from October</b>.

### ✅ If you live with Parkinson's

This does not change anything for you today. But there is one thing worth taking from it —
<b>"the benefit does not last as long as it used to" and "I have to take it so often" are things
that can be worked on</b>, not just endured. Cutting five doses a day down to three was possible
in an actual trial.

If your current medication keeps fading before the next dose is due, that belongs in your next
appointment rather than in the background of your day. Extended-release forms of levodopa exist in
many places, and some people are prescribed a different formulation only at bedtime, when
overnight stiffness is the problem.

<Callout text="One thing to hold on to — an extended-release tablet must never be split or chewed. Breaking it destroys the structure that lets the drug out slowly, so the whole dose is released at once. If a tablet is hard to swallow, do not cut it on your own; say so to your neurologist or pharmacist. And when a formulation changes, the same number of milligrams does not translate one-to-one, so the dose is always readjusted by prescription." />

### 🙋 If you are a care partner

The most useful preparation for news like this is <b>a record</b>. Deciding whether to change the
formulation or the number of doses belongs to the care team, but the raw material for that decision
can only be gathered at home.

Even a few days of these three makes the conversation a different one.

<ul class="cell-list">
  <li><b>How many minutes until the medication takes effect</b> — thirty, or an hour</li>
  <li><b>How early it fades before the next dose</b> — something like "an hour before the next one, the stiffness is back"</li>
  <li><b>Which part of the day is worst</b> — before the first dose, mid-afternoon, or at night</li>
</ul>

"The medication doesn't seem to last as long lately" is much less useful than "the lunchtime dose
is gone in two hours."

## A blood sample, and an estimate of cognitive decline and freezing of gait years later

### 🔍 How this came about

Researchers in South Korea analysed blood from <b>541 people with Parkinson's</b> and <b>180
healthy controls</b>, publishing in the journal npj Parkinson's Disease. The samples came from
PPMI, a large long-running observational dataset based in the United States.

What they looked at was not gene <b>variants</b> but gene <b>activity</b>. Two people can carry the
same genes while those genes are switched on to different degrees, and that degree can be read in
blood. Rather than examining 29,000 genes one by one, the team grouped them into <b>bundles that
switch on and off together</b>. Two bundles turned out to be strongly tied to Parkinson's, and both
were crowded with genes involved in <b>inflammation and immune cells</b>.

From those two bundles they gave each person a score between 0 and 1, where a higher score means
further from the pattern seen in healthy controls. Then they looked at what happened over a
<b>median of eight years</b>.

Even after accounting for age, disease duration and other factors, a higher score came with a
<b>7.3-fold higher risk of cognitive impairment and a 3.4-fold higher risk of
[freezing of gait](/en/lifestyle/freezing-of-gait-tips/)</b>.

The most interesting part, though, is <b>what it did not connect to</b>. The score had <b>no
relationship at all</b> with wearing off or
<Term brief="Involuntary writhing or swaying movements that can develop after long-term levodopa use, as the brain's response to the drug changes. They tend to appear when the medication is at its strongest — during 'on' periods." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-are-dyskinesia-and-motor-fluctuations-on-off">dyskinesia</Term>.
Those two come from how the body responds to dopamine medication. Cognitive decline and freezing of
gait are on the other side of that line — the side dopamine alone does not explain — and the
inflammation signal in blood lined up with exactly that side.

### ✅ If you live with Parkinson's

A "7.3-fold" figure can be alarming, so here is the situation stated plainly: <b>this is not a test
you can ask for at an appointment.</b> It is a score calculated from research data, and it still
has to be confirmed in independent groups.

It is worth knowing for a different reason. It adds to the evidence that <b>memory problems and
feet that freeze are not the result of taking medication wrongly</b>. However carefully you take
your dopamine medication, those symptoms need to be managed on their own terms.

<Callout text="This study analysed existing records from 541 people and has not yet been validated in an independent group. And a figure like this is not a prediction about any one person — it means that, on average, the group with higher scores went on to experience more of these problems. There is no way to find out your own score today, and no separate prescription that follows from knowing it." />

### 🙋 If you are a care partner

What this study offers day to day is <b>a sense of what to watch for</b>. Counting what actually
happened over those eight years, freezing of gait showed up in <b>74.5%</b> and cognitive
impairment in <b>22.0%</b>. Neither is rare.

Freezing of gait in particular <b>leads straight to falls and yet rarely comes up at
appointments.</b> It often happens only at home ("just when he crosses the threshold"), and the
walk down a clinic corridor goes perfectly well.

If there was a moment when the feet stuck, write down <b>where it happened and what they were about
to do</b>, and pass that on as it is — whether it was a narrow doorway, a turn, or trying to do
something else at the same time changes what helps.
Our article on [freezing of gait](/en/lifestyle/freezing-of-gait-tips/) sets out things you can try
at home.

## What you can do now

The two stories look unrelated but meet in one place — <b>Parkinson's is not one problem, and there
is no single response to it</b>. Medication fading sooner is something formulation and dose
frequency can work on. Cognitive decline and freezing of gait have to be looked after outside what
dopamine medication reaches.

There is one thing you can do today. Write down, for a few days, <b>when the medication starts and
when it fades</b>, and <b>when the feet stuck or something slipped your mind</b>. Both of those make
the next appointment a far more specific conversation.
