This time we're covering two research developments aimed at slowing or stopping disease
progression itself, not just easing symptoms. One is a stem cell therapy designed to replace
damaged dopamine neurons outright, which has just received fast track status in the US. The
other is basic research that, for the first time, pinpoints the pathway toxic protein uses to
enter healthy neurons. Neither is close to being an actual treatment yet, but both point to
where future therapies may be headed.

## A ready-to-use stem cell therapy that replaces damaged dopamine neurons gets FDA fast track status

### 🔍 How this came about

<b>XS411</b>, a stem cell therapy developed by the Chinese company XellSmart, has received fast
track designation from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA). XS411 is a cell therapy that
uses induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) to pre-manufacture dopamine-producing neurons, which
are then transplanted into damaged areas of the brain — a "ready-to-use" or off-the-shelf format,
meaning cells don't need to be freshly cultured for each person. It received US FDA
investigational new drug (IND) approval in January 2025, and Chinese regulators cleared clinical
trials that April; enrollment for a
<Term brief="The stage that gathers preliminary data on whether a drug actually works, while safety checks continue." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-do-phase-1-2-and-3-mean-in-clinical-trials">Phase 2 trial</Term> in China has already been completed.

This fast track designation was supported by results from the
<Term brief="The earliest stage of a clinical trial, which checks whether a drug is safe. It usually runs in a small number of people and looks at safety before effectiveness." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-do-phase-1-2-and-3-mean-in-clinical-trials">Phase 1 trial</Term> — participants who
received the transplant tolerated it well with no serious side effects, the duration of benefit
from their existing medication (levodopa) lengthened, motor symptoms became less severe, and
quality of life improved. Brain imaging confirmed that the transplanted cells survived and were
producing dopamine.

### ✅ For you

If the benefit from your current medication is wearing off sooner and sooner, this is exactly the
problem this kind of stem cell therapy ultimately aims to address. That said, it's still in
clinical trials, and there's no timeline for if or when it might become available here.

<Callout text="Phase 1 trials are an early stage that confirms safety in a small number of participants. This fast track designation only speeds up the FDA's review process — it doesn't mean the treatment's effectiveness has been proven. Phase 2 results will tell us much more about how well it actually works." />

### 🙋 For your care partner

"Stem cell therapy" can sound like a finished treatment, but this is a surgical procedure that
involves transplanting cells directly into the brain, and it's still in clinical trials. If you're
curious about clinical trials recruiting here, checking the [Clinical Trials](/en/clinical/) menu
now and then is one way to stay informed.

## Yale researchers identify how a toxic protein first gets into neurons

### 🔍 How this came about

Researchers at Yale University tested more than 4,400 proteins found on the surface of neurons
and identified two — mGluR4 and NPDC1 — that act as the entry pathway that misfolded
<Term brief="A protein in nerve cells. Clumps of it, misfolded, are believed to damage neurons and drive Parkinson's disease. Antibody treatments and vaccines aimed at clearing it are an active area of research." href="/en/lifestyle/glossary/#what-is-alpha-synuclein">alpha-synuclein</Term>
uses to get inside healthy neurons, publishing the findings in the journal Nature Communications.
Both proteins were also confirmed to be present in dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia
nigra, the brain region most affected by Parkinson's.

When researchers blocked the function of these two proteins in mice, the spread of toxic protein
into neurons dropped sharply and disease progression slowed markedly. Dr. Stephen Strittmatter,
who led the research, said developing a drug targeting mGluR4 in particular is realistic — mGluR4
is already a target used in drug development for other conditions, which could help speed up the
development of a new therapy.

### ✅ For you

This isn't a treatment you can get right now, but it points to the possibility of a future drug
that targets the very "pathway" Parkinson's uses to spread inside the brain. We'll keep covering
news about treatments aimed at slowing progression as it develops.

<Callout text="This is basic research done in mice, and it will take considerable time before any drug based on these findings could be tested in people. A drug targeting mGluR4 still has many stages to go through before it could actually be developed and tested." />

### 🙋 For your care partner

If you've heard that "Parkinson's spreads through the brain," this research is the first to show,
in concrete detail, exactly what pathway that happens through. Findings like this basic research
are what eventually build toward real new treatments, so it's worth keeping an eye on how this
area develops.

Both of these are aimed at slowing or stopping disease progression itself, but the stem cell
